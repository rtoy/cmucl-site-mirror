Documentation: Branch Cuts for Special Functions

Introduction

Most of the inverse special functions have branch cuts. The CLHS defines where the branch cuts are and the continuity of the branch cut. However, CMUCL defines some of these differently, following Kahan's definitions instead.

Branch cuts are complicated and analysis takes quite a bit of work. Hence, we follow Kahan as much as possible to define the special functions appropriately on the branch cuts.

Continuity

CLHS definitions

The CLHS says \(\sin^{-1} z\) has a branch cut on the real axis for \(x < -1\) and is continuous with quadrant II and a branch cut for \(x > 1\) which is continuous with quadrant IV.

The branch cuts for \(cos^{-1} z\) are on the real axis for \(x < -1\) which is continuous with quadrant II and a branch cut for \(x > 1\) which is continuous with quadrant IV.

Kahan's Definitions

Kahan's uses this the idea of Counter-Clockwise Continuity (CCC) to define continuity:

Attach each slit ot whichever side is approached when the finite branch-point at its end is circled counter-clockwise.

Definitions of Special Functions

We gather here the definitions of the special functions that we are interested in. For functions where the branch cut is on the imaginary axis, we can ignore because CL doesn't have pure imaginary numbers. Every number with an imaginary part must also have a real part.

However, this isn't true for real numbers. We have explicit real numbers, and the complex numbers whose imaginary part may be a signed zero. This complicates things.

To start, let's define the special functions 

$$
  \begin{align}
    \log z &= \log\left(|z|\right) + i \arg z \\
    \sqrt{z} &= z^{1/2} \\
    \sin^{-1} z &= -i \log\left(iz+\sqrt{1-z^2}\right) \\
    \cos^{-1} z &= \frac{2}{i}\log\left(\sqrt{\frac{1+z}{2}} 
        + i\sqrt{\frac{1-z}{2}}\right) \\
                &= \frac{\pi}{2} - \sin^{-1} z \\
    \tan^{-1} z &= \frac{\tanh^{-1} iz}{i} \\
    \sinh^{-1} z &= \log\left(z + \sqrt{1 + z^2}\right) \\
    \cosh^{-1} z &= 2 \log\left(\sqrt{\frac{z+1}{2}} +
      \sqrt{\frac{z-1}{2}}\right) \\
    \tanh^{-1} z &= \frac{\log(1 + z) - \log(1-z)}{2}
  \end{align}
$$

Compare these to the definitions given by the CLHS: 

$$
  \begin{align}
    \sin^{-1} z &= -i \log\left(iz+\sqrt{1-z^2}\right) \\
    \cos^{-1} z &= \frac{\pi}{2} - \sin^{-1} z \\
    \tan^{-1} z &= -i\log\left(\left(1+iz\right)
      \sqrt{\frac{1}{1+z^2}}\right) \\
    \sinh^{-1} z &= \log\left(z + \sqrt{1 + z^2}\right) \\
    \cosh^{-1} z &= 2 \log\left(\sqrt{\frac{z+1}{z}} +
      \sqrt{\frac{z-1}{2}}\right) \\
    \tanh^{-1} z &= \frac{\log(1 + z) - \log(1-z)}{2}
  \end{align}
$$

We note that these basically match Kahan's definition except for \(tan^{-1} z\). In this case, Kahan specifically mentions this definition and says he chooses the "more perspicuous definitions".

Operations

To evaluate these special functions, we must take care not to unnecessarily destroy signed zeroes. In particular, Kahan says

mixed-mode arithmetic between a real and complex number is presumed NOT to be performed by coercing the real to complex, but rather in a way that avoids unnecessary hazards like \(0\infty\) or \(\infty-\infty\) by avoiding unnecessary real operations

Thus, for real \(\beta\) and complex \(z = x + iy\), operations must be done as follows: 

$$
  \begin{align}
    \beta + z &= (\beta + x) + iy \\
    \beta z   &= \beta x + i\beta y \\
    \frac{z}{\beta} &= \frac{x}{\beta} + i\frac{y}{\beta} \\
  \end{align}
$$

but 

$$
  \begin{align}
    \frac{\beta}{z} &= 
      \begin{cases}
        \dfrac{\beta}{x+\dfrac{y}{x}y} -
          i\dfrac{y}{x}\left(\dfrac{\beta}{x+\dfrac{y}{x}y}\right)
          & \mathrm{if} \, |y| \le |x| \\
        \dfrac{x}{y}\left(\dfrac{\beta}{y+\dfrac{x}{y}x} -
          i\dfrac{\beta}{y+\dfrac{x}{y}x}\right) 
          & \mathrm{if} |x| \le |y|
      \end{cases}
  \end{align}
$$

Also multiplication or division by \(i\) should be accomplished not by actual multiplication bu rather by swaps and sign reversals: 

$$
  \begin{align}
    iz &= -y + ix \\
    i\beta + z &= x + i(\beta + y) \\
    (i\beta)z &= i(\beta z) \\
    \frac{z}{i\beta} &= -i\frac{z}{\beta} \\
    \frac{i\beta}{z} &= i\frac{\beta}{z}
  \end{align}
$$

Note also that \(-z\) is not \(0-z\) but rather \(-z = -x - iy\). Thus \(-(+0 + iy) = -0 -iy\) instead of \(+0 -iy\) which is what happen with \(0-z\).
